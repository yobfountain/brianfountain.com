The pilot

Global Business Solutions is TikTok's sales organization. Its account managers, sellers and ops leads were the audience for most of the training my team built, and they had one thing in common: they spent all day in a vertical feed. Journey was an internal pilot that took that seriously. Instead of pulling people out to a course, it delivered training in the format they already knew by heart: full-screen, one idea at a time, swipe for the next.

📱

The phone beside this text is a working recreation of the pilot: the watch-and-check loop, the optional stitch, and the journey board. The original code stayed with TikTok, so the demo is rebuilt from scratch with a stand-in still where a video would play.

Bite-sized, and built for the learner's role

Every lesson ran under a minute and taught exactly one thing. That constraint did most of the design work for us. A skill like giving feedback breaks naturally into a short series: why vague praise fails, the situation-behavior-impact shape, delivering it live, receiving it well.

The series was the same for everyone. The examples were not. The pilot knew the learner's role, and the scenario inside each lesson was written for that role, so the same skill arrived wearing familiar clothes:

Account Manager

The feedback is about a client pitch. The stitch prompt is a manager's throwaway comment on a deck.

Ops lead

The feedback is about a handoff that slipped. The stitch prompt is a peer's vague note on a process doc.

Nobody had to translate a generic example into their own work. That was the point.

Watch and check, then an optional stitch

The core loop was two swipes: watch, then check. A third swipe offered something extra to anyone who wanted it.

  • Watch. A short video with the same chrome as the app: caption, sound, the rail on the right. Nothing signals "this is training" except the lesson tag and the role chip.
  • Check. One question, answered by tapping. Instant feedback, and a sticker if you got it right. The check is on the very next swipe so the idea is still warm.
  • Stitch, if you want to. A third slide, styled like a challenge, invited the learner to stitch their own response. It was never required, and swiping past it cost nothing. Stitching was the app's native way to reply to a video with a video, so practicing the skill meant using the feature people already used to be funny with their friends.

For the people who took it up, the stitch turned into a weekly competition. Entries were visible to the team, the best three were featured on Monday, and that small stage did more for participation than any reminder email.

The journey board

Progress was a board of stickers. Each one was earned by doing something specific, watching a first lesson, spotting the SBI shape, stitching an entry, holding a streak, and the locked ones showed what was still ahead. Stickers, rather than points or percentages, because stickers are a thing you collect and a thing you show. In the demo, answering the check correctly and tapping the stitch button both unlock stickers on the board, which is how the pilot behaved.

What the board changed was completion. A course you abandon halfway leaves nothing behind. A board with five of eight stickers on it is a reason to come back.
