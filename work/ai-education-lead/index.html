AI FLUENCY · EDUCATION

Fluency is the judgment that still works after the tools change

I have taught career changers to code, built technical learning for a worldwide sales organization whose products changed every month, and spent the last two years building products on the same models people now need to learn. This page covers how I would teach people to work with AI, and the work behind it.

Fluency was the product, and employment was the measure

At General Assembly I taught more than 30 classes and workshops and was consistently the highest-rated instructor. At New York Code and Design Academy I was Lead Instructor of the immersive bootcamp, where fluency was what students paid for and a graduate's job offer was the grade. At TikTok the audience was a global sales organization, and the subject was an ad product suite that changed several times a month.

All three taught me the same lesson. Tactics copied from a tutorial stop working the week the tool updates. What lasts is the underlying model of how the thing behaves, so that's what I teach, and mastery is what I assess. The specifics can be regenerated as often as they need to be. AI tools change faster than anything I've taught before, which only makes that approach more useful.

Curriculum that works without me in the room

I have designed adult learning for audiences of very different sizes: courses for Google's advertiser audience, the redesign of Thinkful's free public Prep program, and nearly five years of technical learning across TikTok's Global Business Solutions. The TikTok work was asynchronous, consumed across regions and time zones, and paired with measurement that showed whether any of it stuck. Much of it was delivered by a partner facilitator team, so every session had to carry enough of the expert's knowledge for someone else to teach it.

At NYCDA I did the same for instructors. I wrote the teacher-training and curriculum-design processes that let people I never met teach to our standard while the school grew from three locations to seven. The Claude Code skills I write now do that job for agents: the standard is written down, so the work happens to spec without me watching. A modular library that other teams can remix and re-teach is the same idea at a larger scale.

Concrete first, then the abstraction

I've spent my career making technical ideas concrete. I built a linear algebra course around drone light shows, made neural-network visualizations that let people see what a model is doing, and taught Rails for years to students with no programming background to lean on. Before any of that I wrote comedy and narrative at UCB, in games, and in transmedia projects, which is where I learned what keeps an audience in its seat.

I also make the media myself: scripts, video shot and cut in Final Cut and Character Animator, interactive pieces, and shipped games. At Google I managed outside production partners, including SweetRush, to deliver video and interactive assets. At Mister Face I sat on the agency side of that relationship and delivered creative on client timelines. Editorial style guides I've written are what kept fast agency work consistent.

I build on the models I would be teaching

My products run on large language models, and they stop working without them. Vibeside is a mobile-first IDE that orchestrates Claude Code sessions in the cloud. GenBuzz, a daily publication on AI filmmaking, runs an agent pipeline for ingestion and entity extraction that ends at a human editor, and nothing publishes until I approve it. I'm a Rails developer who also writes production Python, including a LoRA training API on GPU infrastructure, and I deploy and run my own servers.

That matters for teaching because the hard parts of AI fluency are the ones you only meet in production. Agents can be confidently wrong. A review step has to be designed before it's needed. A pipeline that drafts in minutes still needs a person who knows what good looks like. I have hit each of those in my own systems, and those failures are the examples I teach from.

Positions argued in writing, with a way to be proven wrong

At TikTok I wrote internal papers arguing for competency-based learning: small, tracked pieces of knowledge, and less weight on time-spent metrics in favor of mastery. The same papers proposed starting at course-level granularity, because the competency model I wanted wasn't practical on the data model we had yet. It was a two-phase compromise, and I said so. I also built the measurement that could check me, with competency lift and Kirkpatrick evaluation tying training to actual sales results.

My M.Ed in instructional design is from Western Governors University, one of the early schools to run on competency-based education. The essays at brianfountain.com/writing carry the same argument forward.

New practices, built from nothing

At NYCDA I built the corporate training line from scratch and taught it to Fortune 500 clients, including Walt Disney Parks and Resorts Technology Group and Estée Lauder. At TikTok I introduced the competency-based learning practice and carried it from the papers that made the case through the data model to the measurement system. That happened during hypergrowth, when ad policy and product training changed several times a month and whole courses went out of date. These days I'm a solo founder, so every process I work inside is one I built.
