TECHNICAL INSTRUCTION

Teaching engineers, with the code open

For more than a decade I have taught technical work: people changing careers and writing their first line of Ruby, engineers at Disney and Estée Lauder learning on paid time, a worldwide sales force whose product shifted monthly. Over those same years I shipped production software, and after Claude Code launched in February 2025 I started building my products on Claude. What follows covers that teaching, the instructors I have prepared to do it, and the systems that hold both accountable.

More than a decade of teaching technical material live

I taught over thirty classes and workshops at General Assembly, covering HTML, CSS, the command line, Ruby on Rails and Git, mostly to adults who showed up with no programming background, and I was consistently the highest-rated instructor there. At New York Code and Design Academy I was Lead Instructor of the immersive bootcamp, where the thing being sold was fluency and the measure was whether graduates got hired. I also built the school's corporate training line and delivered it to Walt Disney Parks and Resorts Technology Group and Estée Lauder. Those rooms were full of engineers who knew their own systems better than I did, the time was bought by the hour, and the outcome had to be visible in their work the next week.

Not all of my stage work has been classrooms. I have spoken and run workshops at NY Tech Meetup, at SXSW, for Audi, and at conferences around the world, and that is a different room again, a thousand strangers who owe you nothing for the first five minutes. Lately I produced two one-day hackathons, one on educational tools and one on vibecoding, where the job is keeping a whole room building until evening instead of listening for an hour. Most of my live hours have been in person, in New York, where I have taught since 2013. I know the distributed version from the design side: for nearly five years at TikTok, the technical learning I built for Global Business Solutions was used in every region and time zone the company sells in, and it had to work without me in the room.

Building in front of the room

I teach engineers by building while they watch. The agent I demo is the same one I run. G3NPRO's Director Agent reads a scene, plans the coverage, and writes shot prompts, and every exchange with Claude runs through structured schemas so what comes back lands in the database as scenes and shots instead of prose. It works the platform through a small set of typed tools, meaning its actions follow the same code paths a person's would. When a senior engineer gets skeptical, the doubt usually settles on those two choices, and the fix is opening the code instead of the slides.

Labs break. Years of bootcamp mornings mean years of setups that died at five past nine, and the response never changes: work the problem down in front of everyone, speaking each step, until the debugging itself turns into the teaching and no one loses their twenty minutes.

The lessons stop landing, too. I figured out how to read a room in a theater long before I could read one in a classroom, and the classroom signal is quieter. It is the same signal, though, and the fix is the same: shift direction while you still have them.

Helping other teachers improve

While I was Chief Creative Officer, NYCDA expanded from three locations to seven, and no school grows by relying on whichever instructors happen to be in the building. I wrote the teacher-training process and the curriculum-design process, so instructors I never met were able to teach the program to our standard as we grew. I was responsible for the curriculum, the mission and values, and the path an instructor took to become ready to teach, all while teaching the flagship program myself. The person who set the bar was on the schedule too, and that kept the bar believable.

At TikTok, I designed sessions that a partner facilitator team delivered, so each one I wrote had to contain enough of the teaching for someone else to stand it up. Now I do the same for agents, building Claude Code skills that encode my production standards so they apply them without my oversight. Putting the standard in writing so another person can teach to it remains the part of the work I value most.

My background before teaching was years of improv and sketch comedy at UCB, and no part of my training gets used more in a classroom. Improv is rehearsal for the thing you did not plan. A scene goes somewhere nobody meant it to go, and your job is to handle what is really happening rather than the version you had in your head. A broken lab or a question from the back row that knocks the lesson sideways is that same problem, and when the moment permits, joking about what just went wrong is often the quickest way to settle a room. Listening before answering is the other thing improv drilled into me. On stage you absorb the full offer before responding, and with students that means catching what a learner is truly stuck on, which is almost never what they asked about. Running a room takes people skills at least as much as subject knowledge, and I treat them as the same job. That habit carries into how I observe other instructors: sit through the entire session, then offer the single note that will make the next one different. Knowing a subject and teaching it well are different skills, and I would sooner push a session back than sign off on someone who cannot yet win back a room they have lost.

Putting sessions together with experts, then reworking them based on what happened in the room

At Google I worked with the Global Curriculum Manager for Measurement and Attribution to make a dry subject into courses that, in the partner's words, read more like a story than a formal training while still conveying the meaningful information. At TikTok the experts were product managers whose ad products changed monthly, and the format had to hold up under that. Journey, an internal pilot there, is my clearest case of improving from what actually happened: the featured-entry stage lifted participation more than any reminder email, so the design attention went to the stage and the emails were cut. I rebuilt the pilot as a working demo you can swipe through.

Checking whether any of it worked

I built Kirkpatrick Level 4 measurement alongside a data scientist at TikTok. Quarterly cohort analysis linked what people consumed to what they sold, while competency lift provided a direct capability metric. Leaders could see their teams' progress in real time through a dashboard connected to the LMS. The instrument could also prove me wrong, which is the only kind worth building. Applied to instruction, this approach reveals which sessions build capability and which merely generate attendance.

Production software, and the person who runs it

I am a Rails developer who also writes production Python, and I operate the systems I build. G3NPRO is a production platform with animation studio customers: a factory routes each generation request to one of more than a dozen provider adapters, a strategy resolver picks the technique based on the assets on file, and characters get their own LoRAs trained through a Python API I built on Modal GPU infrastructure. Vibeside is a control plane for coding agents where each task runs in its own git worktree on your machine, and the panel sends commands and displays the results. I deploy and run my own servers. Architecture, implementation, debugging, and tradeoffs are the conversation I have with myself every day, and I am glad to have it with a staff engineer instead.

Working with Claude since Claude Code came out

I built everything above on Claude, starting with Claude Code's release in February 2025. The Director Agent runs on Claude with structured outputs and typed tools. Vibeside orchestrates Claude Code in headless mode, and Claude Code carries a large part of how I ship, with skills I authored to enforce my standards. Chapters, my novel-outlining tool, defaults to a small browser-based model but switches to Claude on request, with the tradeoff stated right at the toggle. I build directly against the Claude API and self-host open models on Modal, so I have seen hosting from both angles. The tools change monthly. I run GenBuzz, a publication covering AI filmmaking, to keep pace; covering the field daily means watching it move.

Someone at the moment of consequence

Every AI system I run puts a human ahead of anything a client or a reader sees: shot review in G3NPRO, editorial approval on GenBuzz, the needs-review state in Vibeside. Chapters keeps a writer's draft on their own machine unless they pick otherwise, and spells out what that choice costs. I design verification into the system, since I have watched agents be confidently wrong inside my own pipelines. That is what I carry into a room of engineers learning to build with a model. The enthusiasm is genuine, and so are the gates.
