CURRICULUM DESIGN · ENABLEMENT

Every lesson starts from something a manager could watch someone do

I have written curriculum for TikTok's worldwide sales organization, for Google's advertisers, for career changers learning to code, and for engineers at Fortune 500 companies. The method comes from competency-based education. Decide what a capable person does, write it down as behavior someone could observe, and build backward to the lesson, the check and the refresher. Each step below comes with the work behind it.

From what good looks like to behavior you can watch

Curriculum starts before there is any content. With a team's leaders I write down what a fully proficient person in each role actually does: the calls they run, the questions they ask, the mistakes they've stopped making. Then I rewrite each item until a manager could watch for it on a real call. "Understands the product" fails that test. "Explains a reporting change in the client's own metrics before the client asks" passes. Proficiency levels come from the same list, so moving up a level means showing more of the behaviors. Finished modules don't count toward it.

I introduced this practice at TikTok for Global Business Solutions, the company's sales organization. It comes from my training: my master's in instructional design is from Western Governors University, which built its degrees on competency-based education, where students advance by demonstrating mastery. ADDIE is the process I was trained in, and competency mapping is how I do its analysis step.

Learning paths by role, for the first month and every month after

TikTok's competency system assembled a learning path for each person from their region, their sales vertical and the size of their clients. New hires got the onboarding sequence for their role. Everyone else got ongoing training as the ad products changed, which happened several times a month, plus an on-demand library for whatever question was in front of them that afternoon. Leaders followed their teams' progress on a dashboard connected to the LMS. For some teams facilitator-led training dropped 25% once the paths replaced how they took training.

Objectives first, then the assessment that checks them

I don't consider an objective finished until I can write the assessment item for it. If I can't say what a correct answer looks like, the objective is still vague. For behavior on the job, scenarios make the best assessments. The learner gets a situation from their own week and picks what they'd do, and the feedback explains what each choice would have led to.

Practice and refreshers that follow the evidence

One course rarely changes how someone works. Journey, a pilot I designed at TikTok, delivered practice inside the vertical feed salespeople already used all day. Every lesson ran under a minute and taught one idea, and the knowledge check came on the next swipe. An optional weekly stitch competition gave people a reason to practice where colleagues could see it, and a sticker board gave a half-finished week a reason to be finished. The featured-entry stage raised participation more than any reminder email did.

Refreshers should be scheduled by evidence. If the behavior shows up on real calls, the refresher is wasted time for that person. If it doesn't, they need it right away, before the gap becomes a habit.

With experts, and with the people who deliver it

Most of my content started in someone else's head. At TikTok the experts were product managers whose ad products changed several times a month. At Google it was the Global Curriculum Manager for Measurement and Attribution, who said my course read "more like a story than a formal training, while still conveying meaningful information." Running that work means agreeing early on who owns what. The expert owns accuracy, I own whether a learner can use it, and both of us are held to a date. With SweetRush it meant holding an agency to a style guide on an agency timeline.

The content also has to survive being delivered by someone else. At TikTok I designed sessions that a partner facilitator team delivered, so each one had to carry enough of the teaching for a facilitator to run it without me. At NYCDA I wrote the teacher-training and curriculum-design processes, which is how instructors I never met delivered the program to our standard as the school grew from three locations to seven.

Written for the selling method the team already uses

TikTok's sellers worked from the Challenger model. A Challenger seller opens with an insight that changes how the client sees their own business, tailors it to the person in the room, and takes control of where the conversation goes. Product training for that team was most useful when it ended in something a seller could teach a client. MEDDICC, Command of the Message and Sandler each ask for something different, and the principle carries over: learn the method the team is coached on, then write the scenarios in its vocabulary, so practice and coaching point the same direction.

After launch: accuracy, and the learner data

Content starts going stale the day the product moves. My answer at TikTok, argued in internal papers, was granularity. Knowledge is broken into small pieces that are tracked individually and updated or retired one at a time, so a product change costs a paragraph instead of a course. For impact I built Kirkpatrick Level 4 measurement with a data scientist. Quarterly cohort analysis tied what people consumed to what they sold, and competency lift measured capability directly. It showed which content built capability and which only drew attendance, and that decided what we revised next.

AI in the curriculum workflow

I've built on Claude since Claude Code came out in February 2025. I also run GenBuzz, a daily publication where agents ingest sources and draft each story, and nothing publishes until I approve it. Curriculum work fits the same arrangement. A model drafts the video script from an expert's notes, sketches the storyboard, writes a first pass of scenario questions with their wrong answers, and sorts open-ended learner feedback into themes. I write the objectives and check every assessment item against them myself, because a model will happily write a confident answer key for a question that has two right answers.
