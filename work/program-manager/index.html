TECHNICAL ENABLEMENT · PROGRAM MANAGEMENT

New engineers should know when they're ready, and so should their managers

For nearly five years I built the technical learning for TikTok's worldwide sales organization, whose ad products changed every month. Before that I wrote the teacher-training and curriculum processes that carried a coding school from three locations to seven. Each time the job was to get people ready quickly and keep what they read accurate after the product moved on. This page covers how I run that program for a technical team, and the work it draws on.

Onboarding that ends when someone is ready

At TikTok I replaced a course catalog with a competency model. Each competency was something a person could be observed doing, and the system assembled a learning path for each person from their region, their sales vertical and the size of their clients. Being ramped stopped meaning hours watched. It meant the competencies for your role had been shown, and your manager could see which ones were left. Leaders read their own teams' progress on a dashboard connected to the LMS, and some teams cut facilitator-led training by 25%.

For an engineering team I start the same way. I sit with the leads and write down what a fully ramped person in each role does in an ordinary week, then work backward to the first thirty days. The new hire gets a path with a visible end. Their manager gets a list of what's left instead of a hunch.

A knowledge base with owners and review dates

TikTok also showed me what happens to documentation when the product changes monthly. Each release invalidated whole courses, and nobody could say with confidence which version was current. My answer, argued in internal papers, was granularity: break knowledge into small pieces that are tracked individually, give each piece an owner, and update or retire them one at a time. A product change should cost a paragraph. It shouldn't cost a course.

Applied to an engineering knowledge base, that starts with an audit. Find the duplicates, pick the canonical version of each, and mark the rest deprecated with a pointer to the replacement, so nobody's bookmark leads to a dead end. After that every document carries a named owner and a review date, and someone checks the dates. Readers at different levels also need different ways in. A new hire and a three-year veteran can share the same canonical page and still shouldn't be sent to it first.

A regular cadence, aimed at measured gaps

Onboarding ends, and the product keeps shipping. The cadence I run has live sessions for material that needs questions answered, short async updates for everything else, and release summaries written for the person who has to explain the change to a customer the same day. What goes into that cadence should come from evidence about where people aren't ready yet.

At TikTok I built that evidence with a data scientist. Kirkpatrick Level 4 measurement tied what people consumed to what they sold through quarterly cohort analysis, and competency lift measured capability directly. It showed which content built capability and which only drew attendance, and it was set up so it could have shown we were wrong. When a format wasn't working, we changed the format. Journey, a pilot I ran there, moved training into the vertical feed salespeople already used all day. Lessons ran under a minute, with a knowledge check on the next swipe.

Working alongside the leaders, translating both ways

Most of my enablement work has been translation. At TikTok the experts were product managers and the audience was a sales force, so technical material had to become something a seller could use on a call that afternoon. At New York Code and Design Academy it ran the other way. I built the school's corporate training line and taught engineers at Walt Disney Parks and Resorts Technology Group and Estée Lauder, people who knew their own systems better than I did. Adapting go-to-market material for engineers is the same skill pointed the opposite way: cut the pitch, and add the detail an engineer is going to check.

Getting knowledge out of experts is the other half. At Google I worked with the Global Curriculum Manager for Measurement and Attribution to turn a dry subject into courses that, in the partner's words, read more like a story than a formal training. At TikTok I wrote sessions that a partner facilitator team delivered, so each one had to carry enough of the expert's knowledge for someone else to teach it.

Programs from nothing to launch, several at once

The competency practice at TikTok began as internal papers making the case. It became a data model, then a measurement plan, then a system the organization used every day. At NYCDA, while the school grew from three locations to seven, I wrote the teacher-training and curriculum-design processes so instructors I never met could teach to our standard, and I taught the flagship program the whole time.

Now I run four products in parallel as a solo founder. G3NPRO is used in production by animation studios. GenBuzz is a daily publication, and Vibeside and 1K Notes round out the set. Keeping four things moving at once depends on written specs and status anyone can read, plus a willingness to drop whatever isn't earning its time.

AI for drafting, people for sign-off

I have built on Claude since Claude Code came out in February 2025. GenBuzz runs an agent pipeline that ingests sources and drafts each story with attribution, and nothing publishes until I approve it. I write Claude Code skills that encode my standards so agents apply them without me watching. In an enablement program the same tools draft release summaries from changelogs, flag documents whose source code changed since their last review, and turn a recorded expert session into a first draft. A person still owns every page that ships. I have watched agents be confidently wrong in my own pipelines.
