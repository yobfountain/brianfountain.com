COST MODEL · UNIVERSAL RICE & BEANS

What UNYC would cost

The essay puts free rice and beans for every New Yorker at about $316 million a year. This page builds that number from the bottom up, using USDA's own purchase prices, the per-pound fees states pay distributors, and New York City payroll data. Each input below cites its source and can be edited.

How the model works

It is an activity-based cost model, the method Margolies and Hoddinott used at IFPRI to compare food and cash transfers in four countries. You list each thing that has to happen for a pound of rice to reach a kitchen, price each one from the best public number available, and add them up. They found that, per transfer, food cost two to four times as much as cash to deliver, almost entirely because of storage, handling and transport.

The model has five cost blocks:

  • Food, priced as packed and delivered by the truckload to a city warehouse. The anchor is USDA's own estimated delivered price for the same products it buys for food banks and tribal programs.
  • Packaging premium for the printed UNYC bags over USDA's standard pack.
  • Distribution from the warehouse to stores, pantries and vendors, priced per pound from what states pay contract distributors for USDA Foods.
  • Leakage, a percentage of everything above, for food that is resold, hoarded or wasted.
  • Administration: city staff priced from the payroll dataset with IBO's fringe rate, then lot inspection at USDA's published fees, IT, audits, outreach, a legal reserve, mobile trucks and pickup lockers.

Two costs sit outside the annual total because the essay's design pushes them elsewhere: the handling that stores absorb, and one-time start-up spending. Both are estimated further down, and the handling cost can be switched into the total.

Each input carries a tag. Sourced means the central value comes straight from a published number. Inferred means it is arithmetic on published numbers, with the steps in the note. Assumption means I could not find a public figure and picked a range.

Checking it against real programs

A bottom-up model can be internally consistent and still wrong, so the outputs are set beside programs that already move food or benefits at scale. None of them works exactly like UNYC.

MeasureUNYC (central)Benchmark

Two of these comparisons cut against UNYC. Its admin share runs above Fair Fares and TEFAP. Part of that is real: lot inspection, trucks and lockers are things those programs do not do. Part is scale, since UNYC's food budget is smaller than the benefit flows those ratios are measured against. TEFAP's ratio also flatters TEFAP, because food banks raise private money to cover distribution that the federal grant does not. The delivered cost per pound sits well under the city's own emergency food program, which buys a mix that is one-third refrigerated.

Costs outside the annual total

Store handling

The essay has large stores absorb receiving, shelving and checkout. Priced per bag, that work costs little. A clerk stocks somewhere around 75 to 110 cases an hour, and a $0 scan adds a few seconds at the register. New York's bottle bill pays stores 3.5 cents per container for a comparable chore.

What the model leaves out

  • Tariffs and trade policy. Jasmine and basmati are imported. The aromatic price range assumes today's freight and duties. A large tariff change on Thai or Indian rice would move that input past its high value, and it is already one of the biggest drivers.
  • Crop shocks. U.S. rice prices are high after a 39-year-low crop (ERS). The food lines are spot prices from autumn 2026. A real budget would hedge with multi-year contracts or carry a price reserve.
  • Correlation. The simulation treats inputs as independent. In practice a bad rice year raises domestic and imported prices together, so the true spread is somewhat wider than the simulated one.
  • Offsets. Nothing here nets out savings elsewhere: rice and beans the city's Community Food Connection no longer needs to buy, or pantry demand that falls. Those are real but would need their own estimate.
  • Take-up. The volume comes from what the city eats, scaled by a take-up share. If the program pulls in people who were not eating much rice before, volume goes up. If a $0 bag sits unclaimed in some neighborhoods, it goes down.
  • Financing. Inventory float is counted as start-up working capital, with no interest cost.

Datasets and sources

Food prices

  • USDA FNS: TEFAP List of Materials, FY26 (spreadsheet). Estimated delivered price per pound, pack size and truck weight for every USDA Food. Rice and pulse rows used here.
  • USDA FNS: CSFP List of Materials, FY26 (spreadsheet) and FDPIR program page, for 1 lb packs.
  • USDA AMS: Weekly National Rice Summary. Milled long-grain at $30.00 to $34.50 per hundredweight, week of September 28, 2026.
  • USDA AMS: Dry bean, pea and lentil grower bids.
  • USDA RMA: 2025 dry bean and dry pea harvest prices.
  • Thai Rice Exporters Association: weekly export prices. Hom Mali about $1,178 and Thai fragrant $687 per metric ton FOB, September 30, 2026.
  • APEDA (India): Rice market dashboard, February 2026. Basmati export prices.
  • USDA ERS: Rice Outlook, September 2026, and the Rice Yearbook dataset.
  • USDA ERS: Vegetables and Pulses data.

Volume

  • USDA ERS: Food Availability (Per Capita) Data System.
  • Choices: Who (Will) Eat Rice in the United States? (2024). Demographic shares of rice demand behind the essay's 1.6x multiplier.

Distribution and handling

  • Wisconsin DPI: USDA Foods product values and fees, SY2026-27. $0.02 admin, $0.07 storage and $0.1115 delivery per gross pound.
  • Maine DOE: USDA Foods distribution. $3.25 storage plus $4.90 delivery per case of dry goods.
  • NY OGS: USDA Foods for schools, 2023. $3.17 per case assessment; about 75% of program cost is warehousing and distribution.
  • Mercer County Food Bank: Member agency guidebook, 2025. Feeding America shared maintenance fee capped at $0.19 per pound.
  • U.S. Census Bureau: Annual Wholesale Trade Survey, operating expenses, 2022 (spreadsheet). Grocery wholesalers spend 11.7% of sales on operations.
  • NYS DEC: Bottle bill. 3.5 cent handling fee per container.
  • van Zelst et al.: Logistics drivers for shelf stacking in grocery retail, International Journal of Production Economics (2009).
  • Catholic Charities Dallas: Mobile food pantry operations. About $250,000 a year per truck program, including food.
  • ParcelHive: Outdoor parcel locker costs. Vendor estimate, low reliability.
  • NYC DOT: LockerNYC pilot. No per-unit cost disclosed.

Staff and administration

  • NYC Open Data: Citywide Payroll Data (dataset k397-673e), FY2025 base salaries by civil service title.
  • NYC IBO: Analysis of the November 2025 Financial Plan. 42% fringe rate.
  • NYC IBO: Expanding Fair Fares, June 2026. $6.9 million admin on an $89.8 million program, broken out by staffing, advertising, IT, mailing and other.
  • USDA AMS FGIS: Directive 9180.74, fee schedule effective October 1, 2025. Rice and pulse inspection, lot and lab fees.
  • NYC Comptroller: Audit of MyCity system development, December 2025. More than $100 million spent across 120 agreements.
  • City Record: Citywide financial statement audit contract, FY2024-27. $21.8 million over four years.
  • NYC Council: HRA budget report, March 2026. Community Food Connection budget and staffing.
  • NYC Mayor's Office of Food Policy: CFC FY25 Impact Report. 47.1 million pounds for $51.9 million.
