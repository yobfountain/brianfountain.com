LEARNING DESIGN

Rise of the Super Competency

BRIAN FOUNTAIN · OCTOBER 2026

Congratulations, you've launched your training and it's already out of date. Every large learning and development team knows this story, because every learning and development professional has lived it at least once.

Leadership decides the sales org needs a new certification. A program is scoped. Subject matter experts get pulled off their real jobs for interviews. You engage your internal media production team, or a vendor is hired to produce video. Someone builds a 100+ slide deck explaining the launch plan for the forty-module course. Months go by. Review cycles stack up. Finally it ships, there's a kickoff email from the most senior executive who is gettable, and thousands of people start clicking through it.

Within weeks, a learner in module six notices that the screenshot doesn't match the product anymore. A setting moved. A feature got renamed. A policy changed the week after the content was locked. Somebody posts about it in a team chat, and the correction lives there, in a thread, forever.

Nobody fixes the course. Opening it back up would mean reconvening the experts, re-cutting video, re-running review, and re-publishing to the LMS. It took the whole department to build it the first time. So it sits, collecting small errors, until the next planning cycle comes around. By then there are so many inaccuracies that patching feels pointless, and the decision gets made to throw it out and build a new one from scratch. And the training department is doomed to repeat this cruel Sisyphean loop until the heat death of the universe.

This is not theoretical. I have observed it in many organizations over the years. It isn't a failure of effort or talent. The people involved are usually very good. It's a failure of the unit we design in.

The course is the wrong size

Let's talk about granularity. Products and jobs change in small pieces. A button moves, an eligibility rule changes, a new ad format gets added. Courses are built in big pieces. When the thing you're teaching changes a little at a time and the thing you're maintaining can only be changed all at once, drift is guaranteed.

Drift has a cost beyond the wrong slide. Learners generally assume their training is up to date and accurate, but this trust exists on a sliding scale. Once someone catches an outdated screenshot, they start wondering what else is wrong, and they go ask a coworker instead. The training stops being the source of truth, even for the parts that are still accurate.

So I've been thinking about curriculum differently. I've been thinking about granularity. What's a meaningful bit of learning and how do you manage it? I've been calling the unit a super competency.

The shape of a super competency

A super competency is a self-contained chunk that holds everything needed to document, teach, and assess one thing a person should be able to do. Each one carries:

  1. A title
  2. A description
  3. A Bloom's Taxonomy definition: the observable behavior that shows someone has the competency
  4. A learner-facing learning objective
  5. Learning materials, in whatever media fit
  6. Several assessment options
  7. Links to its parent competencies
  8. A version, a "valid as of" date, a named owner, and a link to the source of truth it depends on (and optimally the person who manages it)

That last field is the one that addresses drift directly. If a competency points at the product doc or policy page it was built from, a change to that page can flag the competency for review. The system finds the stale content before a learner does.

How small should a competency be?

My working rule: a competency is the smallest unit that can be observed on its own and go out of date on its own. If two pieces always change together, they're probably one competency.

The parent links are also a subtle but critical requirement. Competencies don't form a neat tree, because one skill can feed into several bigger ones. They form a directed graph. At the top are terminal competencies, the things the job actually requires. Below them are enabling competencies, the things you need first. (Instructional designers will recognize this as terminal and enabling objectives, carried over to the competency itself.)

Once the structure is a graph, assessment gets efficient. If someone demonstrates a terminal competency, you can reasonably infer they have what it depends on, and you don't need to test every enabling competency one by one. A short adaptive assessment can figure out where a person sits in the structure and send them only to what they're missing.

Two kinds of decay

There are two ways training goes stale, and each needs a different fix.

The first is content drift. The product changed, so the material is wrong. With super competencies, the fix is targeted: update the one affected chunk, bump its version, and ask only the people who completed it to re-verify. Nobody retakes a forty-module course to learn that a menu moved or that this feature is now available in Asia.

The second is learner decay. The material is right, but people forget it. Ebbinghaus mapped the forgetting curve in the 1880s, and spaced repetition has been the answer ever since. What's new is that if every competency is tracked per learner, the system can watch each person's curve and prompt them at the right moment, on the specific thing they're about to lose. Keeping these separate is what makes the reporting accurate and timely.

What leadership gets

Most leaders are asking their training departments to answer a very simple question: "Do our employees have the knowledge they need to do their job?" It's a simple enough question, but it's often really hard to answer confidently. You have new hires who are getting up to speed. You have overly prescribed training, where office managers find themselves watching videos on brand safety. It's very easy for a leader to say "everyone needs to know this" when a reasonable person would object. But the sad fact is we rarely have the tools needed to customize training to the learner.

Most organizations measure training with completion rates. A completion rate tells you who clicked through a course at some point. It can't tell you whether that course is still true, or whether anyone remembers it. You can't trust course ratings, because generally people are nice and know a real person made it, and writing negative feedback could be used against them in a review. Learners are not incentivized to object to training either. New hires don't actually know if the learning objectives are relevant to their role. Seasoned employees who realize the training is irrelevant have calculated the cost-benefit ratio of complaining and made the decision to finish the training and get back to work.

When a product ships a change or a role takes on a new responsibility, it opens a gap between what a role requires and what the workforce has verified it can do. I think of this as readiness debt. Every product release or organizational change adds some. Targeted updates and re-verification pay it down. With competency-level tracking, leaders can see how much debt the org is carrying and how fast it clears, by team and by role.

That's a different picture than the one most high-volume orgs have now, where product changes go out as announcements in a messaging app or as quiet edits to existing docs, and nobody knows who saw them.

The same structure works for roles. Take a job description and break it down into observable tasks, and each task maps to competencies in the graph. When the job changes, you add the new competencies and retire the ones that no longer apply. The training changes as fast as the role does. This also brings a dose of reality to recruiters and hiring managers who turn the job description into a wish list of capabilities. So many role descriptions are divorced from what employees are doing on a day-to-day basis.

It also turns learners into part of the maintenance team. Put a "this is out of date" button on every chunk. People on the front line usually notice product changes before L&D does. The report goes to the named owner, who can fix one chunk in an afternoon. Better still, owners who watch their source docs can update before anyone has to report anything.

Assessment that keeps up

Assessments drift too, and good assessments are expensive to write. This is where AI changes the math. A system can generate alternate assessment items for a competency, watch how learners perform on them, and keep generating until the items are properly calibrated, then retire the ones that stop separating people who have the skill from people who don't.

The more interesting shift is in what can be assessed at all. The standard objection to breaking curriculum into small pieces is that real expertise doesn't come in small pieces. A good sales call isn't fifteen separate skills performed in sequence. That objection used to be hard to answer at scale, because judging a complex performance took a human evaluator.

It doesn't anymore. A learner can now run a simulated sales call against an AI customer and get feedback the moment it ends. I built a working prototype of this in a day with ElevenLabs' agent framework. An agent like that can grade against the Bloom's definitions already sitting in each competency, and a single realistic performance can serve as evidence for many competencies at once.

The part that's finally possible

The promise of education technology has always been dynamic assessment and training fitted to the person: find out what someone knows, teach them what they don't, and skip the rest. Microlearning got the size of the content right but usually lacked the structure underneath to know which small piece a given person needed.

The pieces to do this properly exist now. Competencies small enough to update, a graph that tells you how they connect, assessments that can be generated and calibrated, and AI that can judge a real performance.

What has to change is the habit of designing training as a monument. Build it in pieces that can be swapped out the week the product changes, and the yearly teardown stops being necessary.
